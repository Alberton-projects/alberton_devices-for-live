# The Max for Live devices in *Alberton Multiverse*

Read out of the saved set on 2026-08-04: **32 device instances, 12 distinct devices**,
ten of them written here and two from elsewhere. Notes for writing about them later.

| Device | Where it sits | Copies | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drum Mapper | Kick, Snare, HiHat, Cymbals | 4 | Folds any note onto one drum's articulations |
| Bass Mapper | Bass Synth, Bass Electric | 2 | Folds any note into the bass register |
| Step Sequencer v4 | Pad 1, Piano 2, Lead 2 | 3 | Four independent step sequences per track |
| Step Sequencer (fixed) | Bass Synth | 1 | Same device, older copy, reached via a preset |
| PC Kit Selector Receiver | seven melodic tracks | 7 | Turns a broadcast number into a program change |
| Kit Selector V4.3 | MIDI REC | 1 | The brain: one control surface for the whole rig |
| Transpose Q | MIDI REC | 1 | Queues a transposition, applies it on the downbeat |
| Gamepad v80 | Live Scratcher | 1 | A game controller as an instrument |
| Dummy Tempo Automator | 29 DUMMY | 1 | Makes Live's tempo automatable |
| VisualBeat | Main | 1 | A metronome you watch instead of hear |
| Gated Delay | eight group tracks | 9 | Ableton *Creative Extensions*, all bypassed |
| Live Scratcher 1.0.4 | Live Scratcher | 1 | Third-party scratch instrument |

The interesting thing about this collection is that it is not a pile of effects. It is
one instrument, distributed: a control surface (Kit Selector) that talks to seven small
receivers by name, note mappers that make any input playable on any track, and a
transposer that waits for the bar line. Very little of it makes sound.

---

## The mappers — playing the wrong notes on purpose

**Drum Mapper** (`alberton-drum-mapper.js`, 162 lines) sits on each of the four drum
tracks with a menu picking KICK, SNARE, HIHAT or CYMBALS. A note already inside that
instrument's range passes through untouched; anything else is folded onto it. Snare
articulations are weighted — the four common ones are three times as likely as the seven
rare ones — and hi-hat and cymbal choice comes from **velocity**, not pitch: hit hard and
you get an open hat or a crash, play soft and you get a closed hat or a ride. The mapping
is remembered per incoming note so that note-off releases the note that was actually
played.

The idea it embodies: on the drums, *how hard* should choose the sound and *which key*
should not matter at all.

**Bass Mapper** (`alberton-bass-mapper.js`, 65 lines) is the same shape with a simpler
job — fold everything into two octaves from C1 — so any controller, any clip, any
sequencer output lands somewhere playable on a bass. (It does this incorrectly today;
see `REVIEW.md`.)

## The Kit Selector — one panel for the whole rig

**Alberton Kit Selector V4.3** (`live_controller.js`, 743 lines) is much the biggest
thing here and the only one that knows about the set as a whole. It carries a control per
track — Kick, Snare, HiHat, Cymbals, BassE, BassS, Pad1, Pad2, Piano1, Piano2, Lead1,
Lead2 — plus a nine-channel bank of FX macro sliders, and it drives them by three
different mechanisms depending on what is on the track:

- **drum tracks** — selects a chain inside the Drum Rack over the Live API;
- **melodic tracks** — sets the plugin's preset parameter (Analog Lab, Pianoteq,
  Mellotron V), or broadcasts a program change when the plugin only speaks MIDI;
- **FX** — captures and recalls the macro values of each group's rack.

The whole state is stored and recalled with `pattrstorage`, so a "kit" is one recallable
snapshot of the entire instrument. It also stores a **name** per preset slot, and resets
every track volume on recall.

The detail worth telling: **V4.1.1 added a program-change queue** because sending
several PCs at once crashed Analog Lab. They now go out one every 150 ms. That is the
kind of thing you only learn on stage.

**Alberton PC Kit Selector Receiver** is the other half, and it is tiny — twenty objects.
Seven copies sit on the melodic tracks; each has an ID menu picking which of the eight
broadcast names it listens to. It passes MIDI straight through and splices the incoming
number in as a program change. The two devices are wired to each other by name only
(`alberton_pad1`, `alberton_piano2`, …), never by a cable, which is why the receiver can
be dropped on any track and just work.

## Transpose Q — waiting for the bar line

**Alberton Transpose Q** (`alberton-transpose-v2.js`, 106 lines) holds a pending
transposition and does nothing with it until a downbeat arrives, then walks every track
whose name looks melodic (bass, pad, piano, lead, vocoder — but never MIDI REC), finds
the `[PITCH]` device on it, and writes the new value. So you dial the change during a
phrase and the whole rig moves together, in time, on the bar.

Version 2 is named for the fix: v1 detected the downbeat some other way, v2 uses
`plugsync~`.

## Gamepad v80 — a controller with 1779 objects

**Alberton_Gamepad_v80** is the largest patcher in the set. It reads a game controller
through `hi` and exposes, for every physical control, a set of four parameters: a mapping
target, a minimum, a maximum and a toggle — some 200 device parameters in total, covering
sticks, triggers, D-pad, face buttons, touchpad axes, gyroscope and accelerometer.
`slot-fire.js` alongside it scans the set's track names, fills a menu with them, and
fires clip slots and scenes, so the pad can play the set as well as play notes.

There is a `rumble` message in there, unwired — the intent to make the controller buzz
was there and never finished.

## The two quiet ones

**Alberton Dummy Tempo Automator** (v1.3) exists to work around something Live will not
do: the master tempo cannot be automated from a clip. So this device puts a tempo dial on
a dummy track, where a clip envelope *can* reach it, and writes whatever it reads to
`live_set` over the Live API. Thirteen objects, and it unlocks tempo automation for the
whole set.

**Alberton VisualBeat** sits on the Main track and blinks a button on every beat, driven
by `transport` under a `metro 16n`. Its better half is a floating window it can open —
a big beat counter whose font rescales as you resize the window, so it stays readable
from across a stage. Audio passes through untouched.

## Not mine

**Gated Delay** is Ableton's, from the *Creative Extensions* pack: nine copies, one on
each group track and every one of them bypassed. **Live Scratcher 1.0.4** is a
third-party scratch instrument on its own track, fed by the gamepad.
